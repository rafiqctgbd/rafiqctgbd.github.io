Renewals & Expiry Dates

How to Never Miss a Renewal: Passports, Licences, Insurance and Other Expiry Dates

A reminder that arrives on the expiry date has already failed. Here is how to work out how much warning each renewal actually needs.

By Mohammad Rafiqul Islam · Updated August 2026 · 10 min read

Almost everyone who has missed a renewal has the same story. They knew the date. They may even have written it down somewhere. What they did not have was a reminder that arrived early enough to be useful — because by the time a passport actually expires, the renewal you needed to start is already six weeks late.

That is the thing most renewal tracking gets wrong. Expiry dates are not deadlines you act on. They are deadlines you need to have finished acting on.

Why yearly renewals are harder than monthly bills

It seems backwards. A monthly bill demands your attention twelve times a year and an annual renewal only once, so the annual one should be easier. In practice it is the opposite, for three reasons:

  • No rhythm to lean on. Paying electricity every month builds a habit that reinforces itself. A yearly renewal gives you exactly one chance to remember something you last thought about twelve months ago.
  • Nothing arrives to prompt you. Monthly bills send a statement, an SMS, sometimes a collector at the door. Most documents just quietly stop being valid on a date nobody announces.
  • The consequences are delayed. Miss an electricity payment and you notice within weeks. Miss a warranty expiry and you find out eighteen months later, when the thing breaks.

The single most useful idea: work backwards from the paperwork

Ask the wrong question and you get a useless reminder. "When does it expire?" gives you a date. The question that actually matters is: how long does the renewal take, and when do I need to start?

A passport is the clearest example. Suppose it expires in March. Setting a reminder for March is worse than useless, because:

  • Processing can take several weeks, and longer in peak season.
  • Many countries will not let you enter with less than six months of validity remaining — so your passport effectively stops working for travel six months before the date printed on it.
  • You may need supporting documents that have their own lead time.

Which means the genuinely useful reminder for a March expiry lands somewhere around the previous August. Nine months of warning is not excessive here. It is the actual requirement.

The rule worth remembering

Set the reminder for the date you need to start, not the date it expires. Then add a second, shorter reminder closer to the deadline as a safety net — because the first one will sometimes arrive in a week when you genuinely cannot act on it.

How much lead time each renewal actually needs

These are practical starting points rather than rules. Adjust them to how long things take where you live, since processing times vary a lot by country and by season.

Months of warning

  • Passport — 6 to 9 months. Driven by the six-month validity rule for international travel, not by the printed expiry date.
  • Visa — 2 to 3 months. Especially if it is tied to employment or study, where a lapse has consequences well beyond a fee.

About a month

  • Driving licence — 1 to 2 months. Often requires a medical certificate or a test appointment, both of which need booking.
  • Vehicle fitness certificate, tax token and registration — 1 month. Driving on an expired one is a fine, and the queue is rarely short.
  • Insurance premiums — 1 month. This matters more than people assume: a lapsed health or life policy can mean losing accumulated benefits, not just paying late.
  • Trade licence and professional memberships — 1 month. Renewal fees often rise once you are past the date.

A few weeks

  • Domain names and hosting — 2 to 4 weeks. Auto-renew is not a guarantee. It fails silently when the saved card expires, and a lapsed domain can be expensive to recover.
  • Bank and credit cards — 1 month. Mostly so you can update the card on every service that auto-charges it, which is the thing that actually breaks.
  • Product warranties — 1 month before expiry. The most forgotten item on this list, because nobody thinks about a warranty until something has already broken. A reminder a month out is a genuine prompt to get a wobbly appliance looked at while the repair is still free.

Why a single calendar event usually is not enough

Putting the date in a calendar is better than nothing, and for some people it is genuinely sufficient. But it fails in two specific ways worth knowing about.

The first is the one above: the event is almost always set on the expiry date, which is already too late for anything involving paperwork.

The second is quieter. On many Android phones, particularly budget and mid-range models, the system aggressively manages battery by delaying or suppressing background notifications from apps it does not consider important. A calendar alert set eleven months in advance is exactly the kind of notification that can arrive late, arrive silently, or get swiped away half-read among twenty others. For a monthly bill that is survivable, because next month comes around. For a once-a-year renewal, that single missed notification is the failure.

Building the system: four steps

1. Do one inventory, once

Sit down with a cup of tea and list everything you own that has an expiry date. Not from memory — go through your document folder, your wallet, and your email receipts. Most people find between fifteen and thirty items, and are surprised by at least three of them.

2. Record the expiry date and the lead time separately

These are two different pieces of information, and conflating them is the original mistake. "Passport expires 12 March" and "start the renewal by August" both need to exist.

3. Put the reminder somewhere that will still exist next year

A note in your phone's notes app is fine until you change phones. Whatever you use, it needs to survive a device change — which is a real argument for something that backs up, rather than a list that lives in exactly one place.

4. Renew the reminder when you renew the document

This is the step everyone skips. The moment you collect the new passport, set the next reminder. If you wait, you will not do it, and you have just reset the whole problem for another five years.

Three mistakes worth avoiding

  • Trusting auto-renew without a reminder. Auto-renew is a convenience, not a guarantee. It fails silently, and it fails most often when the card behind it has expired — which tends to happen around the same time as everything else.
  • Setting every reminder to the same lead time. Thirty days is right for a fitness certificate and badly wrong for a passport. One default across everything guarantees that some reminders arrive too late to be actionable.
  • Tracking only the expensive things. The costly lapses are often small items: a warranty that ran out a month before the fridge failed, or a domain that lapsed and was bought by someone else.

The short version

Renewals do not get missed because people are disorganised. They get missed because the reminder was set to the wrong date — the deadline instead of the starting point — and because a single alert once a year is a fragile thing to hang a passport on. Fix those two and the problem mostly disappears.

Frequently asked questions

How far in advance should I set a renewal reminder?

Base it on how long the renewal takes, not on the expiry date. A passport needs months, because processing takes weeks and many countries require six months of remaining validity to let you travel. A fitness certificate or trade licence needs about a month. Set the reminder for the date you need to start.

Why are yearly renewals harder to remember than monthly bills?

Monthly bills build a rhythm and send you a statement. A yearly renewal gives you one chance to remember something you last thought about twelve months ago, with nothing arriving to prompt you.

Is a calendar event enough for tracking expiry dates?

Sometimes, but it breaks in two ways: the event is usually set on the expiry date itself, which is already too late for paperwork, and Android battery optimisation can delay or silence a notification scheduled months ahead. One event with one alert is the most common way renewal tracking fails.

What documents should I be tracking for expiry?

Anything where lapsing costs money or creates legal trouble: passport, driving licence, vehicle registration, fitness certificate and tax token, insurance premiums, trade licence, professional memberships, domains and hosting, warranties, visas, and bank cards. Warranties are the most commonly forgotten.
